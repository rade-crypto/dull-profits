You tap your phone, and a soda drops. Two dollars. Before the owner of this machine keeps a single cent, five other players get paid.

They're the government, the drink company, the card processor, the software company, and the building the machine stands in.

At one of the busiest airports in America, the building takes 59.5 percent of every sale. We'll get to that deal in a few minutes.

Online, vending machines get pitched as easy passive income. Stock a machine with snacks once a week and let the cash pile up.

So we checked the real numbers, from payment data, city contracts and operators' own posted results. The picture is a lot less exciting than the videos, and it shows where the money in this business is moving.

So let's follow the two dollars.

These machines usually belong to an operator, a business that buys machines, places them in other people's buildings, and keeps them stocked.

In 2025, US vending operators brought in more than seven billion dollars. But no single company controls more than 5 percent of that. Even giants like Compass Group and Aramark are just bigger dots on a map full of small, local businesses.

Most of them are built around a route. An operator drives a loop of locations, restocking snacks, pulling cash, and fixing whatever broke since the last visit.

The machines aren't cheap. One supplier sells small countertop machines from about 1,900 dollars. A combo snack-and-drink machine starts around 3,500. A premium elevator machine costs close to 9,000.

Then there's the deal with the location. Plenty of small businesses host a machine for free, because snacks for their staff are the whole point. Busy places know what they're worth. They usually ask for a share of gross sales, commonly 5 to 20 percent.

Sales per machine depend almost entirely on where it stands. A typical site might do 150 to 700 dollars a month. A strong one, up to 1,500. Top spots like hospitals, factories and transit hubs can do 2,000 or more.

Those are sales figures, though. To see what an operator keeps, go back to the two-dollar soda.

Here's how one equipment supplier models a two-dollar soda. It assumes a 6 percent sales tax, a 10 percent location commission, and most customers paying by card.

First, about 11 cents is sales tax. That was never the operator's money.

The biggest slice is the product. Roughly half the pre-tax price, about 94 cents, goes to whoever supplied the drink.

Then the tech companies take theirs. Card processing costs about 9 cents, and the card reader and software subscription about 4 more.

The building takes its 10 percent. That's 20 cents.

That leaves the operator about 62 cents, before paying for gas, labor, repairs, insurance and income tax.

Every restock means driving out, hauling cases, and doing it all again next week. Those costs come out of the 62 cents too.

Run the full numbers on a machine selling 900 dollars a month, and the same supplier estimates the operator keeps about 197. That's roughly a 22 percent margin, at a decent location.

And the better the location, the bigger its cut. Some public contracts go far past 10 percent. One California college charges 28 percent of gross sales. And that airport from the start of this video? Its deal is bigger still.

Card payments do help the operator in one way. In 2025, card customers spent an average of 2.45 dollars per purchase at vending machines on Cantaloupe's payment network, versus 1.57 in cash. Card fees cost a few cents, but card customers spend more. By last year, 78 percent of vending sales on that network were cashless.

So the operator ends up with a thin slice of each soda. The bigger money in vending goes to three kinds of players.

Winner number one is the location. That airport is Los Angeles International. In 2023, it approved a vending deal where the operator pays 59.5 percent of gross sales, or 100 dollars per machine per month, whichever is more. The operator buys the stock and does the driving, and the airport collects for the floor space.

Winner number two is scale. Driving between machines is one of the biggest costs in vending. So an operator with a hundred machines packed into one area beats someone with ten machines scattered across a city. Big operators also buy products in bulk and can bid on huge contracts.

The biggest of them all is Canteen, owned by the food service giant Compass Group. It started in Chicago in 1929 with five-cent candy bar machines. Today Compass Group describes Canteen as the largest unattended retail provider in the United States, a label that covers far more than vending machines.

The third winner runs the payments. Every tap sends a few cents to whoever runs the card reader. Cantaloupe, one of the biggest, tracked more than 600,000 connected machines and kiosks across the US and Canada in 2025. Together they handled about four and a half billion dollars in sales. Cantaloupe earns a slice of each card sale without stocking a single snack.

But some of the biggest changes in this industry are happening inside offices. Big operators are adding micro markets. A micro market is an unlocked mini-store with open shelves, fresh food, and a self-checkout kiosk.

People spend more in them. In 2025, the average vending purchase on Cantaloupe's network was 2.01 dollars. At micro markets it was 3.02. At smart stores, the AI-powered fridges you unlock with a card tap, it was 4.49.

Micro markets on that network sold more than a billion dollars' worth of food and drinks last year, up 13 percent. Smart stores grew from 71 locations to 572 in a single year.

Vending machines still bring in far more money in total, about 3.3 billion dollars on that network in 2025. But the fastest growth is in these newer formats, where people spend more on every visit.

But the passive income videos skip almost all of this. So here's what the first six months looked like for one real beginner.

They shared their numbers on Reddit. They spent 3,200 dollars on two refurbished machines and another 400 on snacks and drinks. One machine sits in an office with about 50 employees. The other is in an auto repair shop.

The office machine brings in about 280 to 350 dollars a month. The repair shop does 120 to 180. After gas, restocking and repairs, they keep about 130 to 150 dollars a month. Their verdict was simple. It's not as passive as people make it sound.

Now do the math on an ordinary location. One supplier estimates a typical site nets about 20 to 175 dollars a month. At that rate, a 3,500-dollar combo machine takes anywhere from a year and a half to more than 14 years to pay for itself. And that's if nothing breaks.

Things do break. That same operator dealt with a jammed bill acceptor and an attempted break-in within their first six months.

Then there's debt. Personal finance host George Kamel reviewed one creator's 3,300-dollar startup budget and gave blunt advice. Save the full amount in cash first. Put the machine on a credit card, and interest eats into those small monthly profits before you see any of them.

And none of this is new. In 1998, the Federal Trade Commission and ten states announced Operation Vend Up Broke. It was a sweep of 40 enforcement actions against companies selling vending business opportunities. The pitch back then was easy routes and big money. Some sellers even paid fake references to vouch for the profits.

The ads have moved from newspapers to TikTok, and the math behind them is the same.

Back to your two dollars. The supplier takes the biggest slice. The building takes its cut, and the best buildings take most of it. The payment companies take a little from every tap. And the operator keeps what's left, if the location is good and nothing breaks.

The people who win in vending own the location, the scale, or the technology. The machine itself is just the box in the middle.
