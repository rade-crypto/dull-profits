## The two-dollar soda

You tap your phone, and a soda drops. Two dollars. Before the owner of this machine keeps a single cent, five other players get paid.

They're the government, the drink company, the card processor, the software company, and the building the machine stands in.

At one of the busiest airports in America, the building takes 59.5 percent of every sale. We'll get to that deal in a few minutes.

Online, vending machines get pitched as easy passive income. Stock a machine with snacks once a week and let the cash pile up.

So we checked the real numbers, from payment data, public contracts and operators' own posted results. The picture is a lot less exciting than the videos, and it shows where the money in this business is moving.

So let's follow the two dollars.

## Who actually owns the machine

These machines usually belong to an operator, a business that buys machines, places them in other people's buildings, and keeps them stocked.

In 2025, US vending operators brought in more than seven billion dollars, according to the research firm IBISWorld. The biggest names are food service giants Compass Group and Aramark. But most of the map is still covered in small, local businesses.

Most of them are built around a route. An operator drives a loop of locations, restocking snacks, pulling cash, and fixing whatever broke since the last visit.

The machines aren't cheap. One supplier sells small countertop machines from about 1,900 dollars. A combo snack-and-drink machine starts around 3,500. An elevator machine, which gently lowers each item instead of dropping it, starts at about 8,650.

Then there's the deal with the location. Plenty of small businesses host a machine for free, because snacks for their staff are the whole point. Busy places know what they're worth. They usually ask for a share of gross sales, commonly 5 to 20 percent.

Sales per machine depend almost entirely on where it stands. The same supplier's guide puts a typical site at 150 to 700 dollars a month, and a strong one at up to 1,500. Top spots like hospitals, factories and transit hubs can do 2,000 or more.

## Following the two dollars

Those are sales figures, though. To see what an operator keeps, go back to the two-dollar soda.

Here's how one equipment supplier models a two-dollar soda. It assumes a 6 percent sales tax, a 10 percent location commission, and most customers paying by card.

First, about 11 cents is sales tax. That was never the operator's money.

The biggest slice is the product. Roughly half the pre-tax price, about 94 cents, goes to whoever supplied the drink.

Then the tech companies take theirs. Card processing costs about 9 cents, and the card reader and software subscription about 4 more.

The building takes its 10 percent. That's 20 cents.

That leaves the operator about 62 cents, before paying for gas, labor, repairs, insurance and income tax.

Every restock means driving out, hauling cases, and doing it all again next week. Those costs come out of the 62 cents too.

Run the full numbers on a machine selling 900 dollars a month, and the same supplier estimates the operator keeps about 197. That's roughly a 22 percent margin, at a decent location, and it's before the operator pays themselves anything for their time.

And the better the location, the bigger its cut. Some public contracts go far past 10 percent. One California community college district, Riverside, gets 33 percent of gross sales. And that airport from the start of this video? Its deal is bigger still.

## Why card payments help

Card payments do help the operator in one way. In 2025, people paying by card or phone spent an average of 2.45 dollars per purchase at vending machines on Cantaloupe's payment network, versus $1.57 in cash. Card fees cost a few cents, but card customers spend more. And 78 percent of vending sales on that network were cashless.

## Winner 1: the location

So the operator ends up with a thin slice of each soda. The bigger money in vending goes to three kinds of players.

Winner number one is the location. That airport is Los Angeles International. In 2023, its board approved a vending deal where the operator pays 59.5 percent of gross sales, or 100 dollars per machine per month, whichever is more. Even the runner-up bid, from Compass Group's Canteen, offered 51.6 percent. The operator runs and maintains the machines. The airport just collects for the floor space.

## Winner 2: scale

Winner number two is scale. Driving between machines is one of the biggest costs in vending. So an operator with a hundred machines packed into one area beats someone with ten machines scattered across a city. Big operators also buy products in bulk and can bid on huge contracts.

The biggest of them all is Canteen, owned by the food service giant Compass Group. It started in Chicago in 1929 with five-cent candy bar machines. Today Compass Group describes Canteen as the largest unattended retail provider in the United States, a label that covers far more than vending machines.

And the big players keep getting bigger. In 2025, the Alabama bottler Buffalo Rock sold its vending business, almost 700 micro markets and more than 7,600 vending locations, to Canteen and its franchise partners.

## Winner 3: the payment network

The third winner runs the payments. Every tap sends a few cents to whoever runs the card reader. Cantaloupe, one of the biggest, tracked more than 600,000 connected machines and kiosks across the US and Canada in 2025. Together they handled about 4.6 billion dollars in sales. Cantaloupe earns a cut of each card sale, plus monthly fees, without stocking a single snack.

And Cantaloupe itself just changed hands. In May 2026, a rival, 365 Retail Markets, finished buying it for about 848 million dollars. Regulators only let the deal through after the company agreed to sell off a competing kiosk business.

## Micro markets and smart stores

But some of the biggest changes in this industry are happening inside offices. Big operators are adding micro markets. A micro market is an unlocked mini-store with open shelves, fresh food, and a self-checkout kiosk.

People spend more in them. In 2025, the average vending purchase on Cantaloupe's network was 2.01 dollars. At micro markets it was $3.02. At smart stores, the locked smart fridges and cabinets you open with a card tap, it was $4.49.

Micro markets on that network sold more than a billion dollars' worth of goods in 2025, up 13 percent. Smart stores grew from 71 locations to 572 in a single year, though together they still sold only about 6 million dollars.

Vending machines still bring in far more money in total, about 3.3 billion dollars on that network in 2025. But the fastest growth is in these newer formats, where people spend more on every visit.

## A beginner's first six months

But the passive income videos skip almost all of this. So here's what one beginner says their first six months looked like.

They posted their numbers on Reddit. They spent 3,200 dollars on two refurbished machines and another 400 on snacks and drinks. One machine sits in an office with about 50 employees. The other is in an auto repair shop.

The office machine brings in about 280 to 350 dollars a month. The repair shop does 120 to 180. After gas, restocking and repairs, they say they keep about 130 to 150 dollars a month. We can't check those numbers, but they fit the supplier's own ranges. Their verdict was simple. It's not as passive as people make it sound.

Now do the math on an ordinary location. One supplier estimates a typical site nets about 20 to 175 dollars a month. At that rate, a 3,500-dollar combo machine takes anywhere from about 20 months to more than 14 years just to pay for itself. And that's if nothing breaks.

Things do break. That same operator says they dealt with a jammed bill acceptor and an attempted break-in within their first six months.

Then there's debt. Personal finance host George Kamel looked at one creator's breakdown of what it costs to start, about 3,330 dollars, and his advice was blunt. Save it in cash. Don't borrow it or put it on a credit card. It's easy to see why. Put it on a card, and the interest starts before the machine sells a single soda.

## Buying a route instead

If you'd rather buy a route that already exists, the usual yardstick is a multiple of what it earns the owner each year, before they pay themselves.

BizBuySell's figures for vending businesses sold between 2021 and 2025 put the middle at about two times those earnings, with a typical sale price of about 83,500 dollars. Asking prices often run higher. One Chicago-area route with 12 machines is listed at 138,000 dollars. The seller estimates it nets about 49,000 a year, so that's nearly three times earnings.

Those are asking prices and seller-reported numbers, not audited results. A careful buyer asks for every machine's sales records before believing any of them.

## Operation Vend Up Broke

And none of this is new. In 1998, the Federal Trade Commission and ten states announced Operation Vend Up Broke. It was a sweep of 40 enforcement actions against companies selling vending business opportunities. The pitch back then was easy routes and big money. The FTC warned that some sellers paid fake references, people it called singers, to vouch for the profits.

The ads have moved from newspapers to TikTok, and the math behind them is the same.

## Who really wins

Back to your two dollars. The supplier takes the biggest slice. The building takes its cut, and the best buildings take most of it. The payment companies take a little from every tap. And the operator keeps what's left, if the location is good and nothing breaks.

The people who win in vending own the location, the scale, or the technology. The machine itself is just the box in the middle. So if you're ever looking at a route for sale, ask which of those three it actually owns.
