## Thirty dollars a month

Thirty dollars a month. Wash your car as often as you like. That's about what members of America's biggest car wash chain paid on average last year. Before the owner of the tunnel keeps a cent, the workers, the landlord, the utilities, the chemical supplier and the brand all get paid. By our estimate, the owner is left with about 6 dollars and 30 cents.
[VISUAL: Real footage: a car rolls into an express tunnel under foam arches. On "6 dollars and 30 cents", a second number in white: "$6.30 to the owner".]

And the money in this business doesn't always go where you'd expect. In 2021, that chain, Mister Car Wash, sold shares to the public at 15 dollars each. This year, its private equity owner bought out the other shareholders at 7.
[VISUAL: Template 14 (`versus`) on a blurred tunnel background: left "June 2021 · IPO · $15 a share", right "May 2026 · taken private · $7 a share". Source tag: "SEC filings: 424B4 prospectus (Jun 2021); 8-K (May 19, 2026)".]

Car washes have been one of the hottest small-business pitches of the past five years. So we went through the filings: annual reports, a bankruptcy case, a franchise disclosure, and the government's own loan data.

Here's where the thirty dollars goes, who ends up holding the bill, and who really comes out ahead.

## Four kinds of car wash

There are four main kinds of car wash: self-serve bays, in-bay automatics at gas stations, full-serve washes where staff clean the inside, and the one behind the boom, the express tunnel. A conveyor pulls your car through a long building, and the vacuums outside are usually free.
[VISUAL: Four quick real clips, about 3 s each, each with a template 37 (`tag`): "Self-serve" (wand spraying a car in a bay) · "In-bay automatic" (gantry moving around a parked car at a gas station) · "Full-serve" (staff wiping an interior, no faces) · "Express tunnel" (car on the conveyor, then free vacuum stalls).]

Tunnels aren't cheap. Tommy's Express, a big franchise brand, puts the cost of one of its standard tunnels at about 5.1 to 7.5 million dollars.
[VISUAL: Footage of a tunnel under construction (steel frame, concrete pad). Template 1 (`num`): "$5.1M – $7.5M" with label "to build one standard tunnel". Source tag: "Tommy's Express Franchise Disclosure Document, 2026, Item 7 (via 1851 Franchise)".]

And the express tunnel runs on one thing: the membership. At Mister Car Wash, memberships made up 76 percent of wash sales in 2025.
[VISUAL: Template 8 (`doc`): Mister Car Wash 10-K, FY2025, Item 1A. Highlighter sweeps "our UWC subscription program accounted for 76% of our total wash sales in 2025". Source tag: "SOURCE: Mister Car Wash 10-K, filed Feb 27, 2026". Then template 22 (`ring`): 76% memberships, 24% single washes.]

## Following the thirty dollars

Now, let's follow the thirty dollars: one member's monthly payment, split the way a mature tunnel's sales get split. It's our estimate, not one wash's books. The costs come from Tommy's Express franchise sites open five years or more, which averaged about 2.2 million dollars in sales in 2024. The rent comes from car wash properties listed for sale to investors. Sales tax is left out.
[VISUAL: Template 8 (`doc`): Tommy's Express FDD 2025, Item 19 table; highlighter on "$2,204,358" (average gross sales, sites open 60+ months). Source line under the Split: "Costs: Tommy's Express FDD 2025 · Rent: B+E NNN listings, Sept 2026 · Equipment reserve: Mister Car Wash 10-K".]

The biggest slice is people. Wages, payroll taxes and benefits come to about 6 dollars and 84 cents.

Chemicals, water and power take 4 dollars and 46 cents. That's soap and wax, water and sewer, and power for the brushes and dryers.

[VISUAL: Split: "Landlord $5.27" (location teal). Tally $10.37.]

On a mature site selling 2.2 million dollars a year, that's about 460,000. That sounds like plenty. But in their first full year, these sites averaged about 1.1 million in sales, half as much, while the rent stays the same and the staff still cost about three quarters as much.

The membership isn't as locked in as it sounds, either. Across more than 3,000 washes tracked by Rinsed, a membership software company, 7.8 percent of members dropped off every month in the third quarter of 2026. So a wash has to keep signing people up just to stand still.

## Winner 1: the land

So who wins? Winner number one is whoever owns the land. Mister Car Wash owns just 63 of its 548 locations. It leases the other 485.
[VISUAL: Template 8 (`doc`): Mister 10-K, Item 2 Properties; highlighter on "we leased 485 locations and owned 63 locations". Then template 22 (`ring`): "485 leased / 63 owned" (label the ring "Properties", so it isn't confused with Mister's 485 express / 63 interior-cleaning split).]

Many of those leases come from sale-leasebacks. The chain builds a wash, sells the land and building to an investor, and rents it back, often for 20 years. In 2025, Mister did nine of these for about 48 million dollars, and booked a 12 million dollar loss on them. In other words, it sold them for less than its own books said they were worth.
[VISUAL: Template 21 (`equation`): "Build the wash → Sell the land and building → Rent it back for 20 years". Then template 8 (`doc`): 10-K lease note, highlighter on "nine sale-leaseback transactions… aggregate consideration of $48,423… net loss of $12,057… initial 20-year terms". Note on screen: "$ in thousands".]

Investors have lined up to be the landlord. Getty Realty, a real estate investment trust, gets 20.4 percent of its rent from car washes.
[VISUAL: Template 16 (`company`, `names`): "Getty Realty · 20.4% of rent from car washes (June 2026)" and "Essential Properties · 220 car wash sites · 15+ year leases" (on screen only). Background: aerial footage of a tunnel wash beside a highway. Source tags: "Getty Realty corporate profile, Q2 2026"; "EPRT 10-Q, Q3 2026".]

## Winner 2: the brand and the equipment

Winner number two sells the brand and the equipment. Tommy's Express does both: its sister company builds tunnel equipment, and 242 of its 272 locations are franchised. A new franchisee pays a 50,000 dollar fee up front, then 4 percent of sales as a royalty, plus 1 percent to a brand fund.
[VISUAL: Footage: conveyor and brush equipment inside a tunnel, no logos. Template 1 (`num`, `items`): "272 locations" → "242 franchised" → "$50,000 fee" → "4% + 1% of sales". Source tag: "Tommy's Express, 2026; FDD 2026, Items 5–6 (via 1851 Franchise)".]

Several big equipment makers belong to private equity firms. Sonny's, which calls itself the world's largest maker of conveyor car wash equipment, is owned by Genstar. And in 2021, the industrial company Vontier paid about 956 million dollars for DRB, which makes checkout and membership software for car washes.
[VISUAL: Template 16 (`company`, `names`): "Sonny's → Genstar", "MacNeil · Ryko · Zep Vehicle Care → National Carwash Solutions → Berkshire Partners", "DRB → Vontier ($955.8M, 2021)"; MacNeil · Ryko · Zep Vehicle Care → Berkshire Partners shown on the card, not read. Background: blurred footage of a pay kiosk at a tunnel entrance.]

They get paid when a wash is built, and every month it runs. Though since 2024, even Vontier has reported lower demand for its car wash products.
[VISUAL: Template 8 (`doc`): Vontier 10-K FY2025, highlighter on "lower demand for our car wash solutions". Source tag: "Vontier 10-K, filed Feb 12, 2026".]

## The private equity bet

Winner number three was supposed to be private equity. Buy hundreds of washes, put them under one brand, sell memberships, and resell the whole company for more.
[VISUAL: Template 18 (`density`): scattered single washes on a map merge into one coloured network. Footage of a row of identical tunnel buildings.]

Mister Car Wash is the textbook case. The private equity firm Leonard Green has owned it since 2014. In June 2021, Mister went public at that 15 dollars a share, which valued its shares at about 4.4 billion dollars. It had 344 locations and 1.4 million members.
[VISUAL: Template 8 (`doc`): the 424B4 prospectus cover, highlighter on "$15.00 per share". Then template 1 (`num`, `items`): "344 locations", "1.4M members".]

By 2025, it had 548 locations, about 2.3 million members, and more than a billion dollars in sales. Then in May 2026, Leonard Green, which still owned about two thirds of it, bought out the other shareholders at 7 dollars a share.
[VISUAL: Template 14 (`versus`): "2021: 344 sites · 1.4M members · $595M sales · $15" vs "2025–26: 548 sites · 2.3M members · $1.05B sales · $7".]

Sales grew by about three quarters. The share price fell by more than half, and that loss landed on people who bought shares from 2021 on. Including its debt, the deal valued the company at about 3.1 billion dollars, around nine times its adjusted earnings before interest, taxes and depreciation.
[VISUAL: Template 20 (`growth`): sales line rising from $595M to $1.05B, share price line falling from $15 to $7. Then template 1 (`num`): "≈ 9× adjusted EBITDA" with "Net to Owner calculation: $3.1B ÷ $345.4M".]

Zips Car Wash didn't make it that far. The private equity firm Atlantic Street Capital bought it in 2020, when it had 185 sites, and grew it to more than 260. In February 2025, Zips filed for bankruptcy in Texas, with about 654 million dollars of debt.
[VISUAL: Template 8 (`doc`): Zips first-day declaration, p. 3; highlighter on "approximately $653.9 million in funded debt obligations". Source tag: "SOURCE: In re Zips Car Wash, LLC, Case 25-80069, Bankr. N.D. Tex., Doc. 14, Feb 5, 2025".]

Its loans had floating rates, and as rates rose and it borrowed more, its yearly interest bill went from 59 million dollars to 93 million in a single year. Members per site doubled, and sales still fell. The filing blames newer washes nearby, slower traffic and a hurricane.
[VISUAL: Template 12 (`bars`): "Interest: $59M → $93M". Then "Sales: $345M → $303M". Then template 8 (`doc`) p. 23, highlighter on "retail traffic veered to newer refurbished sites owned by competitors".]

It came out of bankruptcy in April 2025, owned by its lenders, with about 230 sites. Even a landlord felt it. Getty Realty, which had leased 12 sites to Zips, called it its first tenant credit issue since 2011.
[VISUAL: Template 24 (`quote`): "first tenant credit issue since 2011", Getty Realty, Q1 2025 earnings call. Background: footage of a closed car wash with an empty lot.]

And Driven Brands, which owned Take 5 Car Wash, wrote down its American car washes by about 1.18 billion dollars over two years. Then it sold all 382 of them for 385 million. And 130 million of that was an IOU from the buyer.
[VISUAL: Template 11 (`receipt`): "Write-downs 2023–24: $1,176M" · "Sale price: $385M" · "of which seller note: $130M" · "Sites: 382". Then template 8 (`doc`): Driven Brands 10-K line "$255 million in cash and… a seller note in the principal amount of $130 million". Source tag: "Driven Brands 10-K, FY2024". ## Too many car washes

So what changed? Part of it was interest rates. A big part was simply too many car washes. Zips put it this way, under oath: "Over the past five years, upwards of 900 new car wash sites have opened annually across the United States."
[VISUAL: Template 8 (`doc`): Zips declaration p. 3; highlighter on the full sentence as it's read.]

The real estate brokerage Matthews counts about 12,000 conveyor washes in the US, and says 87 percent of them have another wash within three miles.
[VISUAL: Aerial footage of a suburban highway strip. Template 4 (`routeMap`) style pins: several washes inside a 3-mile circle. Template 1 (`num`): "87% have a rival within 3 miles". Source tag: "Matthews Car Wash Industry Report, Oct 2026".]

Cities have noticed. In May 2026, Warren, Michigan, paused approvals for new car washes and gas stations for up to eight months. In 2024, Brook Park, Ohio, capped car washes at one for every 10,000 residents.
[VISUAL: Template 8 (`doc`): City of Warren notice; highlighter on "approved an UP TO 8-month moratorium"; small line "25 car washes in 35 square miles (WDIV)". Then template 37 (`tag`) on footage of a street with two washes: "Brook Park, Ohio · 1 car wash per 10,000 residents (2024)".]

And lending has pulled back. Approvals of car wash loans backed by the Small Business Administration peaked at 676 million dollars in fiscal 2021. By fiscal 2025 they were down to 272 million. To be fair, car wash loans made before the boom were written off a little less often than the average SBA loan. Loans from the boom years are still too new to judge.
[VISUAL: Template 20 (`growth`) as columns: SBA 7(a) car wash approvals by fiscal year, peak $676M (FY2021) → $272M (FY2025). Then template 14 (`versus`): "Written off, loans from 2010–16: car washes 6.8% · all SBA loans 7.3%". Source tag: "SBA 7(a) FOIA data (as of Sep 30, 2026), Net to Owner analysis".]

## Buying one yourself

Many car washes that change hands aren't 5 million dollar tunnels. They're older, smaller washes, sold through brokers, with fewer staff, no franchise fee, and usually the owner doing the work. Their split looks different.
[VISUAL: Footage of an older self-serve wash with a few bays, an owner-operator's truck parked by the equipment room (no faces).]

According to BizBuySell, the 32 car washes reported sold that way in 2025 went for a median of about 700,000 dollars, a small sample that doesn't say how many included the land. On average, buyers paid about 4.7 times the seller's yearly cash flow, and that cash flow still includes the owner's own pay.
[VISUAL: Template 8 (`doc`): BizBuySell car wash valuation benchmarks page; highlighter on the median sale price. Source tag: "BizBuySell valuation benchmarks, 2025 (32 reported sales)".]

So take a 700,000 dollar wash at that multiple. The seller would put its cash flow at about 150,000 dollars a year. A buyer has to prove it.
[VISUAL: Template 21 (`equation`): "$700,000 ÷ 4.7 ≈ $150,000 cash flow a year (seller's figure)".]

Now the loan. The SBA usually wants at least 10 percent of the total cost up front. Call it 70,000, and more if the deal includes the building.
[VISUAL: Template 21 (`equation`): "$700,000 price − $70,000 down (10%) = $630,000 loan". Source tag: "SBA SOP 50 10 8.1; 13 CFR 120.910 (15% on special-purpose buildings, 504 loans)".]

Borrow the other 630,000 at 8.75 percent, the median starting rate on SBA car wash loans in fiscal 2026. Over ten years, the usual term when the land isn't included, the payments come to about 94,700 dollars a year. That leaves about 55,000 dollars a year, before income tax and equipment. And that assumes the owner works the wash, with no manager to pay.
[VISUAL: Template 9 (`split`) on a blurred self-serve bay: "$150,000 cash flow" → "Loan payments $94,700" (payments ochre) → "Owner ≈ $55,000" (white), small line "before income tax and equipment · owner works the wash".]

If land is part of the deal, that part of the loan can run up to 25 years. Stretch the whole loan that long, and payments drop to about 62,000, leaving about 88,000. But that's fifteen more years of interest.
[VISUAL: Second row of the same Split: "25 years: payments ≈ $62,200 → owner ≈ $88,000". Tag: "SBA 7(a) terms · Net to Owner calculation · round numbers, not a loan quote".]

Then there's the competition. On one operators' forum, a car wash owner warned that a new express wash nearby "will take 20 to 35 percent off the top of your gross." That's one operator's estimate, not a study. But in a 2025 survey of self-serve owners by the trade magazine Auto Laundry News, more than half said express washes had hurt them.
[VISUAL: Template 24 (`quote`): "will take 20 to 35 percent off the top of your gross", "car wash operator, Car Wash Forum, 2025". Then footage of a new tunnel opening across from an older wash.]

And in California, any tunnel or in-bay wash built since 2014 has to recycle, or buy recycled water, for at least 60 percent of its wash and rinse water. That's one more system a buyer there needs working.
[VISUAL: Footage of water spraying in a tunnel, then a reclaim tank. Template 8 (`doc`): California Water Code §10951, highlighter on "at least 60 percent of the wash and rinse water".]

## Who really wins

Back to the thirty dollars. At a franchise tunnel, the workers get the biggest share. The landlord is often paid for twenty years. The brand and the equipment makers get paid when the wash is built, and for as long as it runs. And the owner keeps about 6 dollars and 30 cents, if the members stay and no new tunnel opens down the road.
[VISUAL: The full Split returns, compact, on tunnel footage. Each slice lights as it's named; the owner slice last.]

So the steadiest winners weren't the investors buying up chains. They own the land, own the brand, or got in before the street filled up. If you're ever looking at a car wash for sale, here are the questions the numbers point to. If you're selling one, expect a careful buyer to ask them.
[VISUAL: Template 17 (`columns`): "Own the land" · "Own the brand" · "Got in early".]

Is the land in the price? If not, what does the lease say? How many members actually pay, and how many cancel? Check the wash's software against the bank deposits. What's being built within three miles? The city's planning agendas will show it. What does an equipment technician say about the conveyor and dryers? Has the land had an environmental check, and do the water and sewer permits transfer? And three years of tax returns, not just the seller's spreadsheet.
[VISUAL: Owner's checklist card (template 19, `ranked`, one line per item as it's read): "Land: in the price, or leased?" · "Paying members, checked against deposits" · "New washes within 3 miles" · "Equipment inspection" · "Environmental check and permits" · "3 years of tax returns". Small footer: "Not financial advice".]
