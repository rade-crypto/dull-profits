## Thirty dollars a month

Thirty dollars a month. Wash your car as often as you like. That's about what members of America's biggest car wash chain paid on average last year. Before the owner of the tunnel keeps a cent, the workers, the landlord, the utilities, the chemical supplier and the brand all get paid.
[VISUAL: Real footage: a car rolls into an express tunnel under foam arches. Template 1 (`num`) in the empty left side: "$30 a month" appears on "Thirty dollars a month". Small source line: "Mister Car Wash, 2025 average, Net to Owner estimate from its 10-K". Then a membership sign with "Unlimited" visible (brand logos blurred).]

And the biggest winners aren't the ones you'd expect. In 2021, that chain, Mister Car Wash, sold shares to the public at 15 dollars each. This year, its private equity owner bought out the other shareholders at 7.
[VISUAL: Template 14 (`versus`) on a blurred tunnel background: left "June 2021 · IPO · $15 a share", right "May 2026 · taken private · $7 a share". Source tag: "SEC filings: 424B4 prospectus (Jun 2021); 8-K (May 19, 2026)".]

Car washes have been one of the hottest small-business pitches of the past five years. Memberships, recurring revenue, and big investors buying up chains. So we went through the filings: annual reports, a bankruptcy case, a franchise disclosure, and the government's own loan data.
[VISUAL: Template 8b (`papers`): three source cards fan in, "Mister Car Wash 10-K", "Zips Car Wash Chapter 11 declaration", "Tommy's Express Franchise Disclosure". Then template 15 (`strike`) low on footage of a tunnel: "Recurring revenue. Easy money." struck through.]

## Four kinds of car wash

There are four main kinds of car wash. Self-serve bays, where you hold the wand. In-bay automatics, where you park and the machine moves around you, often at a gas station. Full-serve washes, where staff clean the inside too. And the one behind the boom: the express tunnel. A conveyor pulls your car through a long building, and the vacuums outside are usually free.
[VISUAL: Four quick real clips, 4 s each, each with a template 37 (`tag`): "Self-serve" (wand spraying a car in a bay) · "In-bay automatic" (gantry moving around a parked car at a gas station) · "Full-serve" (staff wiping an interior, no faces) · "Express tunnel" (car on the conveyor, then free vacuum stalls).]

Express tunnels are what investors have been building, and they aren't cheap. Tommy's Express, a big franchise brand, puts the cost of one of its standard tunnels at about 5.1 to 7.5 million dollars.
[VISUAL: Footage of a tunnel under construction (steel frame, concrete pad). Template 1 (`num`): "$5.1M – $7.5M" with label "to build one standard tunnel". Source tag: "Tommy's Express Franchise Disclosure Document, 2026, Item 7 (via 1851 Franchise)".]

And the express tunnel runs on one thing: the membership. At Mister Car Wash, memberships made up 76 percent of wash sales in 2025. It had about 2.3 million members.
[VISUAL: Template 8 (`doc`): Mister Car Wash 10-K, FY2025, Item 1A. Highlighter sweeps "our UWC subscription program accounted for 76% of our total wash sales in 2025". Source tag: "SOURCE: Mister Car Wash 10-K, filed Feb 27, 2026". Then template 22 (`ring`): 76% memberships, 24% single washes.]

## Following the thirty dollars

So let's follow the thirty dollars. We built this from three sources. The costs are what Tommy's Express franchise sites open five years or more reported in the company's 2025 franchise disclosure. Those sites averaged about 2.2 million dollars in sales in 2024. The rent is our estimate, from the average asking rent on car washes listed for sale in September 2026. The money set aside for equipment comes from Mister Car Wash's annual report. Some states, like Wisconsin, add sales tax on top. We'll leave tax out.
[VISUAL: Template 8 (`doc`): Tommy's Express FDD 2025, Item 19 table; highlighter on "$2,204,358" (average gross sales, sites open 60+ months). Source tag: "SOURCE: Tommy's Express FDD 2025, Item 19 (franchisee-reported, unaudited)". Corner label for the whole Split: "Net to Owner estimate".]

The biggest slice is people. Wages, payroll taxes and benefits come to about 6 dollars 84.
[VISUAL: Template 9 (`split`) with template 7 tally (top right "Owner keeps $30.00") on a blurred tunnel background. First slice "Workers $6.84" (new neutral slice colour, set in the style step) leaves the bar; tally drops to $23.16.]

Chemicals, water and power take 4 dollars 46. That's the soap and wax, the water and sewer bills, and the electricity for the brushes and the dryers.
[VISUAL: Split continues: "Chemicals, water, power $4.46" (supplier blue). Tally $18.70. Background: footage of foam applicators and dryers.]

Repairs, and money set aside for worn-out equipment, take another 3 dollars 6.
[VISUAL: Split: "Repairs and equipment $3.06" (supplier blue, lighter). Tally $15.64.]

The landlord takes 5 dollars 27, in rent, property tax and insurance.
[VISUAL: Split: "Landlord $5.27" (location teal). Tally $10.37.]

The brand and the office take 3 dollars 41. That's a 5 percent royalty and brand fee to the franchisor, plus marketing, office and other costs.
[VISUAL: Split: "Brand, office and other $3.41" (new neutral colour). Tally $6.96.]

Card fees and software take about 66 cents.
[VISUAL: Split: "Card fees and software $0.66" (payments ochre). Tally $6.30.]

That leaves the owner 6 dollars 30, about 21 percent. And that's before income tax, and before paying back the money it took to build or buy the wash.

On a mature site selling 2.2 million dollars a year, 21 percent comes to about 460,000. That sounds like plenty. But in their first full year, sites in the same disclosure averaged about 1.1 million in sales, half as much. The rent stays the same, and the staff still cost about three quarters as much. And these are numbers the franchisees reported, not audited results.
[VISUAL: Template 12 (`bars`): "First full year: $1.09M" vs "Year 5+: $2.20M" average sales per site. Source tag: "Tommy's Express FDD 2025, Item 19".]

The membership isn't as locked in as it sounds, either. Across more than 3,000 washes tracked by Rinsed, a membership software company, 7.8 percent of members dropped off every month in the third quarter of 2026. About 4.5 percent cancelled. For 3.3 percent, the card on file failed. So a wash has to keep signing people up just to stand still.
[VISUAL: Template 41 (`months`): a 12-month grid where 7.8% of squares dim each month. Label "7.8% of members lost a month". Source tag: "Rinsed Car Wash Industry Report, Q3 2026".]

## Winner 1: the land

So who wins? Winner number one is whoever owns the land. Mister Car Wash owns just 63 of its 548 locations. It leases the other 485.
[VISUAL: Template 8 (`doc`): Mister 10-K, Item 2 Properties; highlighter on "we leased 485 locations and owned 63 locations". Then template 22 (`ring`): "485 leased / 63 owned" (label the ring "Properties", so it isn't confused with Mister's 485 express / 63 interior-cleaning split).]

Many of those leases come from sale-leasebacks. The chain builds a wash, sells the land and building to an investor, and rents it back, often for 20 years. In 2025, Mister did nine of these for about 48 million dollars, and booked a 12 million dollar loss on them.
[VISUAL: Template 21 (`equation`): "Build the wash → Sell the land and building → Rent it back for 20 years". Then template 8 (`doc`): 10-K lease note, highlighter on "nine sale-leaseback transactions… aggregate consideration of $48,423… net loss of $12,057… initial 20-year terms". Note on screen: "$ in thousands".]

Investors have lined up to be the landlord. Getty Realty, a real estate investment trust, gets 20.4 percent of its rent from car washes. Essential Properties, another trust, owns 220 car wash sites. Its leases typically run 15 years or more, with rent rising about 2 percent a year.
[VISUAL: Template 16 (`company`, `names`): "Getty Realty · 20.4% of rent from car washes (June 2026)" and "Essential Properties · 220 car wash sites · 15+ year leases". Background: aerial footage of a tunnel wash beside a highway. Source tags: "Getty Realty corporate profile, Q2 2026"; "EPRT 10-Q, Q3 2026".]

## Winner 2: the brand and the equipment

Winner number two sells the brand and the equipment. Tommy's Express does both. Its sister company, Tommy Car Wash Systems, builds tunnel equipment. In March 2026, there were 272 Tommy's Express locations, and 242 of them were franchised. A new franchisee pays a 50,000 dollar fee up front, then 4 percent of sales as a royalty, plus 1 percent to a brand fund.
[VISUAL: Footage: conveyor and brush equipment inside a tunnel, no logos. Template 1 (`num`, `items`): "272 locations" → "242 franchised" → "$50,000 fee" → "4% + 1% of sales". Source tag: "Tommy's Express, 2026; FDD 2026, Items 5–6 (via 1851 Franchise)".]

Several of the big equipment names belong to private equity firms. Sonny's, which calls itself the world's largest maker of conveyor car wash equipment, is owned by Genstar. MacNeil, Ryko and Zep's vehicle care line belong to National Carwash Solutions, owned by the Boston firm Berkshire Partners. And in 2021, the industrial company Vontier paid about 956 million dollars for DRB, whose software runs checkouts and memberships at tunnel washes.
[VISUAL: Template 16 (`company`, `names`): "Sonny's → Genstar", "MacNeil · Ryko · Zep Vehicle Care → Berkshire Partners", "DRB → Vontier ($955.8M, 2021)". Background: blurred footage of a pay kiosk at a tunnel entrance.]

These companies get paid when a wash is built, and every month it runs. But they aren't untouchable. Since 2024, Vontier has reported lower demand for its car wash products.
[VISUAL: Template 8 (`doc`): Vontier 10-K FY2025, highlighter on "lower demand for our car wash solutions". Source tag: "Vontier 10-K, filed Feb 12, 2026".]

## The private equity bet

Winner number three was supposed to be private equity. The idea was simple. Buy hundreds of washes, put them under one brand, sell memberships, and resell the whole company for more.
[VISUAL: Template 18 (`density`): scattered single washes on a map merge into one coloured network. Footage of a row of identical tunnel buildings.]

Mister Car Wash is the textbook case. The private equity firm Leonard Green has owned it since 2014. In June 2021, Mister went public at 15 dollars a share, which valued its shares at about 4.4 billion dollars. It had 344 locations and 1.4 million members.
[VISUAL: Template 8 (`doc`): the 424B4 prospectus cover, highlighter on "$15.00 per share". Then template 1 (`num`, `items`): "344 locations", "1.4M members".]

By 2025, it had 548 locations, about 2.3 million members, and more than a billion dollars in sales. Then in May 2026, Leonard Green, which still owned about two thirds of it, bought out the other shareholders at 7 dollars a share.
[VISUAL: Template 14 (`versus`): "2021: 344 sites · 1.4M members · $595M sales · $15" vs "2025–26: 548 sites · 2.3M members · $1.05B sales · $7".]

Sales grew by about three quarters. The share price fell by more than half. Including its debt, the deal valued the company at about 3.1 billion dollars, around nine times its adjusted earnings before interest, taxes and depreciation.
[VISUAL: Template 20 (`growth`): sales line rising from $595M to $1.05B, share price line falling from $15 to $7. Then template 1 (`num`): "≈ 9× adjusted EBITDA" with "Net to Owner calculation: $3.1B ÷ $345.4M".]

Zips Car Wash didn't make it that far. The private equity firm Atlantic Street Capital bought it in 2020, when it had 185 sites, and grew it to more than 260. In February 2025, Zips filed for bankruptcy in Texas, with about 654 million dollars of debt.
[VISUAL: Template 8 (`doc`): Zips first-day declaration, p. 3; highlighter on "approximately $653.9 million in funded debt obligations". Source tag: "SOURCE: In re Zips Car Wash, LLC, Case 25-80069, Bankr. N.D. Tex., Doc. 14, Feb 5, 2025".]

Its own court filing explains what went wrong. Its loans had floating rates, and as rates rose and it borrowed more, its yearly interest bill went from 59 million dollars to 93 million in a single year. Members per site doubled, and sales still fell, from 345 million to 303 million. The filing blames newer washes nearby, slower traffic and a hurricane.
[VISUAL: Template 12 (`bars`): "Interest: $59M → $93M". Then "Sales: $345M → $303M". Then template 8 (`doc`) p. 23, highlighter on "retail traffic veered to newer refurbished sites owned by competitors".]

It came out of bankruptcy in April 2025, owned by its lenders, with about 230 sites. Getty Realty had leased 12 sites to Zips, and Zips rejected 7 of those leases. Getty called it its first tenant credit issue since 2011, and expected to recover about 70 percent of the old rent.
[VISUAL: Template 24 (`quote`): "first tenant credit issue since 2011", Getty Realty, Q1 2025 earnings call. Background: footage of a closed car wash with an empty lot.]

And Driven Brands, which owned Take 5 Car Wash, wrote down its American car washes by about 1.18 billion dollars over two years. Then it sold all 382 of them for 385 million, about a million dollars a wash. And 130 million of that was an IOU from the buyer.
[VISUAL: Template 11 (`receipt`): "Write-downs 2023–24: $1,176M" · "Sale price: $385M" · "of which seller note: $130M" · "Sites: 382". Then template 8 (`doc`): Driven Brands 10-K line "$255 million in cash and… a seller note in the principal amount of $130 million". Source tag: "Driven Brands 10-K, FY2024".]

## Too many car washes

So what changed? Part of it was interest rates. A big part was simply too many car washes. In Zips' own sworn words: "Over the past five years, upwards of 900 new car wash sites have opened annually across the United States."
[VISUAL: Template 8 (`doc`): Zips declaration p. 3; highlighter on the full sentence as it's read.]

One real estate brokerage, Matthews, counts about 12,000 conveyor washes in the US, and says 87 percent of them have another wash within three miles.
[VISUAL: Aerial footage of a suburban highway strip. Template 4 (`routeMap`) style pins: several washes inside a 3-mile circle. Template 1 (`num`): "87% have a rival within 3 miles". Source tag: "Matthews Car Wash Industry Report, Oct 2026".]

Cities have noticed. In May 2026, Warren, Michigan, paused approvals for new car washes and gas stations for up to eight months. Local TV counted 25 car washes in the city's 35 square miles. In 2024, Brook Park, Ohio, capped car washes at one for every 10,000 residents.
[VISUAL: Template 8 (`doc`): City of Warren notice; highlighter on "approved an UP TO 8-month moratorium". Then template 37 (`tag`) on footage of a street with two washes: "Brook Park, Ohio · 1 car wash per 10,000 residents (2024)".]

Even at the biggest chain, growth slowed. Mister's sales at existing locations grew 6 percent in the first quarter of 2025, and just 1.6 percent in the fourth.
[VISUAL: Template 12 (`bars`): Q1 6.0% · Q2 1.2% · Q3 3.1% · Q4 1.6%, label "Mister Car Wash comparable-store sales, 2025". Source tag: "Mister Car Wash quarterly releases (8-K)".]

And lending has pulled back. Approvals of car wash loans backed by the Small Business Administration peaked at 676 million dollars in fiscal 2021. By fiscal 2025 they were down to 272 million. To be fair, those loans haven't gone bad at unusual rates. In the SBA's own data, car wash loans have been charged off a little less often than the average SBA loan. So far, the damage has been at big chains carrying big debts.
[VISUAL: Template 20 (`growth`) as columns: SBA 7(a) car wash approvals by fiscal year, peak $676M (FY2021) → $272M (FY2025). Then template 14 (`versus`): "Charged off, loans from 2010–16: car washes 6.8% · all SBA loans 7.3%". Source tag: "SBA 7(a) FOIA data (as of Sep 30, 2026), Net to Owner analysis".]

## Buying one yourself

Most car washes that change hands aren't 5 million dollar tunnels. They're older, smaller washes, sold through business brokers. According to BizBuySell, the median car wash sold that way in 2025 went for about 700,000 dollars. On average, buyers paid about 4.7 times the seller's yearly cash flow, and that cash flow still includes the owner's own pay.
[VISUAL: Footage of an older self-serve wash with a few bays. Template 8 (`doc`): BizBuySell car wash valuation benchmarks page; highlighter on the median sale price. Source tag: "BizBuySell valuation benchmarks, 2025".]

So take a 700,000 dollar wash at that multiple. Its cash flow is about 150,000 dollars a year.
[VISUAL: Template 21 (`equation`): "$700,000 ÷ 4.7 ≈ $150,000 cash flow a year".]

Now the loan. For a purchase like this, the SBA usually wants at least 10 percent down. That's 70,000. If the deal includes the building, the SBA's 504 program usually asks for 15 percent, because a car wash is a special-purpose building.
[VISUAL: Template 21 (`equation`): "$700,000 price − $70,000 down (10%) = $630,000 loan". Source tag: "SBA SOP 50 10 8.1; 13 CFR 120.910".]

Borrow the other 630,000 at 8.75 percent, the median starting rate on SBA car wash loans in fiscal 2026, over ten years. The payments come to about 94,700 dollars a year. That leaves about 55,000, before income tax and before setting money aside for equipment, and only if the owner works the wash themselves.
[VISUAL: Template 9 (`split`) on a blurred self-serve bay: "$150,000 cash flow" → "Loan payments $94,700" (payments ochre) → "Owner ≈ $55,000" (white), small line "before income tax and equipment · owner works the wash".]

Then there's the competition. On one operators' forum, a car wash owner warned that a new express wash nearby "will take 20 to 35 percent off the top of your gross." That's one operator's estimate, not a study. But in a 2025 survey of self-serve owners by the trade magazine Auto Laundry News, more than half said express washes had hurt them.
[VISUAL: Template 24 (`quote`): "will take 20 to 35 percent off the top of your gross", "car wash operator, Car Wash Forum, 2025". Then footage of a new tunnel opening across from an older wash.]

And there's water. A conveyor wash uses about 30 gallons of fresh water per car, according to an industry study. A hose left running can use 100. In California, any tunnel or in-bay wash built since 2014 has to recycle, or buy recycled water, for at least 60 percent of its wash and rinse water. That's one more system a buyer there needs working.
[VISUAL: Footage of water spraying in a tunnel, then a reclaim tank. Template 12 (`bars`): "Conveyor wash: 30 gal" vs "Hose left running: up to 100 gal". Then template 8 (`doc`): California Water Code §10951, highlighter on "at least 60 percent of the wash and rinse water".]

## Who really wins

Back to the thirty dollars. The workers get the biggest share. The landlord is often paid every month for twenty years. The brand and the equipment makers get paid when the wash is built, and for as long as it runs. And the owner keeps about six dollars, if the members stay and no new tunnel opens down the road.
[VISUAL: The full Split returns, compact, on tunnel footage. Each slice lights as it's named; the owner slice last.]

The winners in car washes tend to own the land, own the brand, or got in before the street filled up. So if you're ever looking at a car wash for sale, here's what the numbers say to ask for.
[VISUAL: Template 17 (`columns`): "Own the land" · "Own the brand" · "Got in early".]

Who owns the land, and what does the lease say? How many members are there, and how many cancel each month? What's being built within three miles? The city's planning agendas will show it. How old are the conveyor, the brushes and the dryers? And three years of tax returns and card processing statements, not just the seller's spreadsheet.
[VISUAL: Owner's checklist card (template 19, `ranked`, one line per item as it's read): "The land and the lease" · "Members and monthly cancellations" · "New washes within 3 miles" · "Equipment age" · "3 years of tax returns and card statements". Small footer: "Not financial advice".]

If you run a wash, tell us in the comments what memberships have really done to your numbers.
