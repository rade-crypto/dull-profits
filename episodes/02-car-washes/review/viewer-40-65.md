# Ep 02 car washes: target-viewer read (age 58, ~$400k to deploy, ex-manager, watches on TV)

Read as heard, not as read. Spoken lines only were judged; [VISUAL] notes used for context.
Constraints respected: no new facts or numbers, calm tone, no personal advice. Edits are in priority order.

## Overall verdict
I'd watch to the end. It doesn't sound like a guru: no "passive income," no course pitch, and it admits when a number is
one operator's opinion or unaudited. Jargon is mostly handled well (EBITDA is spelled out, "comparable-store sales"
becomes "sales at existing locations," SDE is called "cash flow, including the owner's pay," sale-leaseback is
explained in one breath). The AI narrator never claims to be a person; "we" reads as the channel, which is fine.

Where I'd lose the thread: (1) the Split, where six cent-level figures come at me in a row; (2) the equipment-owner
name list (Genstar, Berkshire, MacNeil, Ryko, Zep, Vontier, DRB); (3) the 504 sentence in the buyer example, which
introduces a second loan program and a second down payment and then never uses them. The hook also says "the biggest
winners aren't the ones you'd expect" and then shows a share price halving, so I wasn't sure who had won.

Questions I'd still have at the end, as a buyer: what's left if I hire a manager instead of working it myself? What do
I need beyond the 70,000 down (closing costs, working capital)? Is 700,000 for the business only or with the land?
As someone who knows a seller: there's nothing for them at all. Edit 11 fixes that last one without new facts. The
others need new research, so they're for a later episode, not this script.

## 1. The 504 sentence confuses the worked example (buyer section)
I've just been told 10 percent down. Then a second program and 15 percent come up, and the math goes on at 10. I'd be
left wondering which one applies to me. "504" means nothing when you hear it.

**Current:**
> If the deal includes the building, the SBA's 504 program usually asks for 15 percent, because a car wash is a special-purpose building.

**Replace with:**
> If the deal includes the building, expect to put down more, usually 15 percent, because a car wash is a special-purpose building. Our example leaves the building out.

(Keep "SBA SOP 50 10 8.1; 13 CFR 120.910" in the on-screen source tag. "Our example leaves the building out" is a framing
choice the writer should confirm matches the BizBuySell median; if it's not known, use: "Our example sticks with 10 percent.")

## 2. The hook's "winners" line points the wrong way
"The biggest winners aren't the ones you'd expect," followed by a stock falling from 15 to 7, made me think the
winner was the private equity owner buying cheap. Later the script says private equity was only "supposed to" win.
It also sounds a little like a teaser.

**Current:**
> And the biggest winners aren't the ones you'd expect. In 2021, that chain, Mister Car Wash, sold shares to the public at 15 dollars each. This year, its private equity owner bought out the other shareholders at 7.

**Replace with:**
> And the people you'd expect to do best haven't always done well. In 2021, that chain, Mister Car Wash, sold shares to the public at 15 dollars each. This year, its private equity owner bought out the other shareholders at 7.

## 3. Tell me what the thirty dollars stands for before the method
The setup paragraph lists three sources, two disclosures, a September 2026 rent survey, and a Wisconsin sales-tax
aside. By the end I'd forgotten the question. I also wasn't sure if the $30 was one member's money or the whole wash's.

**Current:**
> So let's follow the thirty dollars. We built this from three sources. The costs are what Tommy's Express franchise sites open five years or more reported in the company's 2025 franchise disclosure. Those sites averaged about 2.2 million dollars in sales in 2024. The rent is our estimate, from the average asking rent on car washes listed for sale in September 2026. The money set aside for equipment comes from Mister Car Wash's annual report. Some states, like Wisconsin, add sales tax on top. We'll leave tax out.

**Replace with:**
> So let's follow the thirty dollars. Think of it as one member's monthly payment, split the way a typical mature tunnel's sales get split. The costs are what Tommy's Express franchise sites open five years or more reported in the company's 2025 franchise disclosure. Those sites averaged about 2.2 million dollars in sales in 2024. The rent is our estimate, from car washes listed for sale in September 2026. The money for equipment comes from Mister Car Wash's annual report. Sales tax is left out.

## 4. Cent figures are hard to hear: say "and ... cents"
"3 dollars 6" heard aloud sounds like "three dollars sixty" or "three-oh-six." The tally on screen helps, but I'm
across the room. Say the cents explicitly in every Split line (also fix in `voiceover-script.md` spelling).

**Current:**
> Repairs, and money set aside for worn-out equipment, take another 3 dollars 6.

**Replace with:**
> Repairs, and money set aside for worn-out equipment, take another 3 dollars and 6 cents.

Apply the same pattern to: "about 6 dollars 84" → "about 6 dollars and 84 cents"; "take 4 dollars 46" → "take 4 dollars and 46 cents";
"takes 5 dollars 27" → "takes 5 dollars and 27 cents"; "take 3 dollars 41" → "take 3 dollars and 41 cents";
"That leaves the owner 6 dollars 30" → "That leaves the owner 6 dollars and 30 cents".

## 6. Membership churn: three percentages in one breath is too many
7.8, 4.5 and 3.3 go by in five seconds. I'd keep one number and the plain-English reasons; the split stays on screen.

**Current:**
> Across more than 3,000 washes tracked by Rinsed, a membership software company, 7.8 percent of members dropped off every month in the third quarter of 2026. About 4.5 percent cancelled. For 3.3 percent, the card on file failed. So a wash has to keep signing people up just to stand still.

**Replace with:**
> Across more than 3,000 washes tracked by Rinsed, a membership software company, 7.8 percent of members dropped off every month in the third quarter of 2026. More than half of them cancelled. For the rest, the card on file failed. So a wash has to keep signing people up just to stand still.

(Add "4.5% cancelled · 3.3% card failed" as a line on the `months` graphic.)
